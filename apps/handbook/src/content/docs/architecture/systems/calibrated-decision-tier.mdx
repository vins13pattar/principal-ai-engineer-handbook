## Problem

Much of what a production AI system sends to a model is not generation. It is a bounded question —
which queue, which tool, is this in scope, is this safe — paid for at generation's latency and price,
and returned as text that has to be parsed back into the answer. A
[decision model](/reference/lookups/decision-models/) answers the bounded question directly, from a
set you define, with a probability over the answers.

That probability is the whole design. Act on the confident answers, escalate the rest, and the
expensive model only sees the traffic that needs it. The architecture works exactly as well as the
probability is honest — and the backing lab found two ways it is not, both silent.

## Requirements

- Answer bounded decisions faster and cheaper than an LLM, without becoming less correct on the
  answers it keeps.
- Escalate the answers it should not keep, at a known and agreed error rate.
- Detect when the probability stops meaning what it meant when the threshold was set.
- Make the question's wording a reviewed, versioned part of the system.

## Constraints

**The threshold is fitted, not chosen.** It is a function of the model, the question's wording,
the number of options, and the traffic. Change any of them and the error rate the threshold delivers
changes, without anything raising.

**Calibration has to be measured on your data.** Vendor accuracy figures come from vendor-chosen
sets — the most cited one is labelled by LLM consensus, so it measures agreement with LLMs rather
than correctness. A threshold needs labelled outcomes from your own traffic.

**Option count is a hard limit, not a tuning knob.** In the backing lab, three options supported a
threshold that held on held-out data; seventy-seven did not support one at all.

## Request Flow

<Mermaid code={decisionFlow} title="Three tiers, one threshold, and the monitor that keeps the threshold honest" />

A request goes to the cheapest tier that can decide it. Rules take what is certain. The decision
model takes what is bounded, but only keeps answers whose top probability clears a threshold fitted
on labelled traffic; everything below goes to an LLM or a person. Kept answers are sampled and
labelled, escalated ones contribute their outcomes, and a monitor re-fits the threshold when the
relationship between stated confidence and actual accuracy moves.

The monitor is not optional. Without it the threshold is a number that was right once.

## Failure Modes

<FailureMode title="A phrasing that is wrong with total confidence">
  In the backing lab, "Is this product review positive?" scored 52.9% on 1,000 human-labelled
  reviews — the majority baseline — with 998 answers stated at 100%. "Does the reviewer like the
  product?" scored 91.2% on the same rows. A tier built on the first wording keeps every answer,
  because every answer clears any threshold, and escalates nothing. Nothing is malformed, nothing is
  slow, nothing raises. The only defence is to evaluate the wording on labelled data before it
  ships, and to treat a wording change as a deploy.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="Confidence that inverts at high option counts">
  At 77 options the lab measured answers stated at 96% being right 64% of the time, while answers
  stated at 9% were right 23%. A threshold on that signal keeps confidently wrong answers and
  escalates some right ones. A single temperature cannot repair miscalibration that runs both ways —
  fitting one left ECE unchanged. Split the decision instead: a coarse choice first, then a fine one
  within the chosen group, so no single question carries dozens of options.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="A threshold carried across a model swap">
  Decision APIs are compatible by design — one vendor's client works against another's endpoint by
  changing a URL. The probabilities that come back are a different model's. A threshold fitted on
  the old model delivers an unknown error rate on the new one, and the lab's synthetic tests show the
  shape: a threshold fitted on calibrated records misses its target on overconfident ones.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="Escalation that never happens">
  The failure above, seen from the escalation side: if the escalation rate drops to near zero, the
  tier has either become very good or stopped being able to tell. Without labelled samples of the
  kept answers, the two look identical.
</FailureMode>

## Scaling

The decision model is cheap per call — the backing lab measured a median of 44 ms per 3-option
question and 203 ms per 77-option one on a laptop GPU — and several questions about the same state
share one pass. What scales badly is not throughput but labelling: the monitor needs fresh labelled
outcomes in proportion to how fast traffic drifts, and those cost human time. Size the labelling
budget before sizing the fleet.

## Security

A decision model used as a guardrail is an attack surface with a probability attached. Inputs
crafted to push a harmful request above the threshold are the decision-tier version of prompt
injection, and they fail closed only if the tier escalates — so the guardrail case wants a high
threshold and a slow path that is genuinely independent. The question wording is configuration an
attacker would like to change; it belongs under the same change control as the policy it encodes.

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff title="Coverage against error">
  Every threshold trades the share of traffic the tier keeps against how often it is wrong on what it
  keeps. On three-way financial sentiment the lab's fitted threshold kept 61% of traffic at 11.0%
  error on held-out data; raising it keeps less and errs less. The right point is a business
  decision about what a wrong kept answer costs against what an escalation costs — not a default.
</TradeOff>

<TradeOff title="One question with many options, or several with few">
  One 77-way question is one call and an unusable probability. A coarse-then-fine split is two calls
  and two probabilities that each have a chance of meaning something. The extra call is cheap; a
  threshold that cannot be fitted is not.
</TradeOff>

<TradeOff title="Hosted against open weights">
  A hosted decision API needs no GPU and no checkpoint, and its probabilities change when the vendor
  ships a new version. Open weights pin the model — the lab's results are tied to one revision —
  and make the calibration yours to measure and keep.
</TradeOff>

## Cost

The tier saves money only on the traffic it keeps. At 61% coverage, 39% of requests pay for the
decision model and the LLM — so the decision call has to be cheap enough that 0.61 × (LLM cost) of
saving outweighs 1.0 × (decision cost) plus the labelling the monitor needs. At low coverage the
tier is a cost, not a saving. Compute it from your own coverage curve, not from a vendor's latency
table.

## Observability

- **Every decision with its probability**, its wording version, and its model revision.
- **Escalation rate over time**, with alerts on movement in either direction — down is as
  suspicious as up.
- **Reliability on labelled samples**, binned: stated confidence against actual accuracy. ECE is the
  summary; the bins are where a one-sided or inverted miscalibration shows.
- **Realised error on kept answers**, against the target the threshold was fitted for.

## Production Deployment

<Checklist title="Before a decision tier keeps its first answer">
  - The question's wording was evaluated on labelled data, and alternative wordings were compared.
  - Option counts are small enough to calibrate; large label sets are split coarse-then-fine.
  - The threshold was fitted on one labelled sample and checked on a held-out one.
  - The target error rate and the cost of an escalation are written down and owned.
  - Kept answers are sampled for labelling continuously, not only at launch.
  - The threshold is re-fitted on a schedule and on any model, wording, or traffic change.
  - A model swap — including to an API-compatible vendor — is treated as a re-fit, not a config change.
</Checklist>

<Warning title="What the backing lab does not cover">
  Fine-tuned models, `score` questions, and hosted decision APIs. Laya's card says fine-tuning drives
  its headline accuracy, so the lab's numbers describe the out-of-the-box model. The architecture's
  rules — fit, hold out, monitor — do not depend on which of these you use; the numbers do.
</Warning>

<LabCallout repoPath="labs/decision-tier-calibration" status="production-shaped">
  Laya 0.4.1 at a pinned revision, zero-shot, on three human-labelled sets: 5,000 decisions,
  committed as numbers. It measures accuracy against baselines, calibration error with fits judged on
  held-out data, reliability by bin, the coverage-error curve, and whether a threshold fitted for a
  target error holds — and it found one rewording that moves a yes/no question from chance to 91%.

## Interview Questions

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="advanced" title="Your decision model scores 94% on the vendor benchmark. Can you skip the LLM for answers above 0.9?">
  Not on that evidence. The benchmark's labels may not be ground truth — the most cited one is LLM
  consensus — and accuracy says nothing about whether 0.9 means 90%. Sample my own traffic, label
  it, check calibration bin by bin, fit the threshold for the error rate I can tolerate on one half,
  confirm it on the other, and monitor it afterwards.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="advanced" title="The tier's escalation rate dropped to almost zero after a prompt change. Good news?">
  Probably not. A wording change can make a decision model confidently wrong — in the backing lab one
  phrasing scored chance with every answer at 100%. A falling escalation rate is consistent with a
  model that improved and with one that lost the ability to doubt itself, and only labelled samples
  of the kept answers tell them apart. Roll back the wording until they do.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="intermediate" title="You need to route among 80 support intents. Would you use one decision-model question?">
  No. At high option counts the probability stops being a usable escalation signal — the lab
  measured 96% stated confidence being right 64% of the time at 77 options, with no threshold
  reaching 10% error. Route coarse-then-fine: a handful of groups first, then the intent within the
  group, each a question small enough to calibrate.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="intermediate" title="Why not just apply temperature scaling and move on?">
  Because a single temperature moves every probability the same way, and real miscalibration is not
  always one-sided. In the lab it improved one case genuinely, made another look better only by
  turning a broken model into an honest coin flip, and left the rest neutral or worse. Measure the
  reliability bins before and after on held-out data; keep it where it helps.
</InterviewQuestion>
