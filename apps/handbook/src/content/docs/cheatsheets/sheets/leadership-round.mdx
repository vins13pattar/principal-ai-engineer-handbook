## Use This When

You are about to be asked about decisions you have made. Read it the evening before, not in the
lobby — the work is checking which decisions you can actually go deep on, and that check takes
longer than the reading.

Depth lives in [Track: Technical Leadership](/interview/tracks/technical-leadership/) and
[Module 14](/learn/modules/14-leadership/).

## The Sequence

**Preparing** — write two or three of your own past decisions in the
[ADR](/adr/) shape: Context, Problem, **Options**, Decision, **Consequences**. Options is the one
that does the work. If you cannot reconstruct what you rejected and why, you have found that out in
private rather than in the room.

**Answering** — the interviewer is checking internal consistency, so build the answer so it holds:

1. **Constraint.** What had to be true — with a number or a named party, not an adjective.
2. **The decision, and that it was yours.** Say what the team did, then what you decided. Undivided
   "we" gets probed, and the probing eats your time.
3. **The options you rejected, and why.** This is what separates a decision from a default.
4. **The cost.** Every real decision traded something. Naming it is the cheapest credibility
   available and most candidates skip it.
5. **The outcome, in a number that is not a duration.** "Six months migrating" is effort. What
   changed is impact.
6. **What would have changed your mind** — and, if it later did, how long the gap was between the
   evidence arriving and you acting.

Then stop and let them dig. Depth on their question beats breadth on yours.

## Quick Reference

**Have these loaded, four questions deep each**

| Situation | What it samples |
| --- | --- |
| A decision hard to reverse | Whether you recognized it as such *at the time* |
| A disagreement you lost | Whether you can separate being overruled from being wrong |
| A decision you reversed | The gap between evidence arriving and you acting |
| Something you standardized | Whether you standardized where it compounds, or on taste |
| An incident you led | Whether the outcome was a mechanism or a culprit |
| Quality degrading quietly | Whether anyone owned the quality signal |
| Work you handed off | Whether you delegated decisions or only tasks |

**Three decisions you owned beats eight you can describe.** If a row makes you reach for a project
where you were merely nearby, that is the finding.

**Disagreement, decomposed** — constraint (resolve it first; most arguments end here) · conclusion
(name the evidence that would settle it and what it costs) · preference (two-way door — concede and
spend the credibility elsewhere).

**Standardize where it compounds** — identity, observability, deployment. Say what you deliberately
left alone.

## Red Flags

- An account with no stated cost. Either the decision was trivial or it has been sanded smooth.
- Undivided "we" — reads as modesty or concealment, and the interviewer cannot tell which.
- Depth that runs out one question early. The fix is fewer examples, not more polish.
- "We discussed it and aligned on the best approach." A non-answer, and the most common one.
- Impact measured in effort: duration, headcount, lines changed.
- Treating this as the round that needs no preparation. Recall under follow-up is a different task
  from recall, and the difference shows within four minutes.
- Claiming you have never been wrong, or being unable to say how long it took you to notice.
