## Problem

"Is the platform healthy" is a question most teams answer by looking at a dashboard and forming an
impression. That does not scale, does not automate, and does not survive being asked at 3am by
someone who did not build the system.

An error budget replaces the impression with a number that can be spent, alerted on, and acted upon.
But the number is only useful if three consumers agree on it: the alerting that decides whether to
wake someone, the autoscaler that decides whether to add capacity, and the incident automation that
decides how far to escalate. When those read separate signals — which is the default, because they
are usually built at different times by different people — they disagree during exactly the
incidents where clarity matters most.

AI workloads add two wrinkles. The actuator is slow: adding capacity takes minutes because weights
must load, so the control loop has a long dead time. And the most important SLI is often *quality*,
which no infrastructure signal captures.

## Requirements

- **An SLO derived from user impact**, not from what is easy to hit.
- **Burn-rate alerting** that distinguishes a blip from a sustained burn and clears promptly.
- **One severity signal** consumed by alerting, scaling, and incident response alike.
- **Capacity response that reads severity**, not just utilization.
- **Runbook automation** that escalates rather than retrying, with a bounded attempt per step.
- **An honest terminal state** when automation is exhausted.
- **Quality as a first-class SLI**, alongside availability and latency.

## Constraints

- **Burn rate needs two windows.** A single window cannot both suppress blips and clear quickly —
  those requirements pull in opposite directions on window length.
- **The actuator has minutes of dead time.** Any control loop whose correction takes longer than the
  incident it is correcting will oscillate rather than stabilize.
- **An error budget is only meaningful over a defined window.** "99.9%" without a window is not an
  SLO; the window determines how much failure is affordable and how fast it accrues.
- **Quality cannot be measured by infrastructure.** Availability and latency SLIs are free from the
  serving layer; a quality SLI must be constructed deliberately and is always a proxy.
- **Automation acting during an incident changes the incident.** Anything the platform does
  automatically becomes a variable a responder has to reason about.

## Request Flow

<Mermaid code={controlLoop} title="One severity signal drives both the scaling decision and the incident response" />

Request outcomes feed a rolling window, pruned to the SLO's budget window. Burn rate is the observed
error rate divided by the rate the budget permits — 1.0 exhausts the budget exactly at window end.
Each alert rule evaluates that rate over a **long and a short window**, and fires only when both
exceed its threshold: the long window filters noise, the short window lets the alert clear within
minutes of the burn stopping.

The resulting severity is read by both the autoscaling controller and the incident runner. Because
both derive it from the same computation, they cannot disagree about how bad things are.

## Failure Modes

<FailureMode title="Single-window burn-rate alerting">
  One window forces a choice between two bad outcomes. Short, and every transient blip pages someone
  and then flaps. Long, and a genuine fast burn goes unnoticed for most of its duration, then keeps
  alerting long after it stopped because the stale average has not decayed.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="Cooldowns that suppress the response an outage needs">
  A cooldown exists to stop an autoscaler flapping on noisy samples. Applied uniformly, it also
  delays the capacity response to a real, fast-burning outage — the one case where waiting is
  actively harmful. The control needs an explicit escape hatch for the case it was never designed to
  slow down.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="Utilization-only scaling that cannot see why load is high">
  Utilization does not distinguish "traffic grew" from "the service is failing and client retries are
  inflating load". In the second case, adding capacity feeds a retry storm. Severity is what
  disambiguates them, which is why the scaling decision should read it.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="Runbook automation that retries instead of escalating">
  Retrying a failed remediation assumes the step's execution was the problem. Far more often the step
  was the wrong remediation — a restart does not fix a capacity problem — so retrying burns the
  incident's clock while producing no new information and no audit trail of who was told.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="Incident automation with no exhausted state">
  Automation that loops, or that stops silently when its steps run out, withholds the single most
  important fact from the responder: nobody has fixed this, and it is now yours. An honest terminal
  state is a feature, not an admission of failure.
</FailureMode>

<FailureMode title="An SLO nobody derived from users">
  A target chosen because it is comfortably achievable produces a budget that never depletes, alerts
  that never fire, and a false sense of health. The failure is invisible precisely because
  everything looks green — the instrument was calibrated to always read fine.
</FailureMode>

## Scaling

- **The SLI pipeline must outscale the service it measures.** Instrumentation that degrades under
  load blinds you exactly during an incident, which is the one time the measurement matters.
- **Burn-rate windows are queries over retained data**, so window length sets retention and query
  cost. A 30-day budget window with 5-minute resolution is a real storage decision.
- **Per-service SLOs multiply**, and a platform with dozens needs SLO definitions to be data rather
  than code, or the alerting configuration becomes the bottleneck.
- **Scaling actuation is bounded by cold start** (see the
  [Model Serving Platform](/architecture/systems/model-serving-platform/) page), so the controller's
  cadence must exceed the actuator's latency or it will act on stale state.
- **Incident automation must be idempotent**, since a runbook step may fire twice under a retry, a
  partition, or a second alert for the same underlying cause.

## Security

- **Runbook steps act with privilege.** Automation that restarts services or scales infrastructure
  holds credentials that a compromised alerting path could exercise — the trigger is as
  security-relevant as the action.
- **Escalation targets are personal data** and an availability map of the organization; a leaked
  on-call schedule tells an attacker exactly when response is thinnest.
- **Incident records contain the worst-case data**, since they capture systems at their least
  redacted and are retained the longest.
- **SLI pipelines carry production traffic characteristics**, and request-level telemetry frequently
  contains user content that needs redaction before it becomes metrics.
- **Automated remediation needs an audit trail** matching any human action — what fired, why, with
  what authority, and what it changed.

## Trade-offs

<TradeOff title="Multiwindow alerting vs. a single threshold">
  Two windows per rule buys noise suppression and fast clearing simultaneously, which no single
  window achieves. The cost is configuration complexity — each severity needs a threshold and two
  window lengths — and rules that are harder to explain to whoever is paged by them. The complexity
  is worth it because the alternative is either alert fatigue or blindness, and both erode trust in
  the alert itself.
</TradeOff>

<TradeOff title="Automated remediation vs. paging a human immediately">
  Automation resolves common causes in seconds and, when it fails, has consumed time and changed
  system state before a human arrives. Paging immediately is slower for routine failures and gives
  the responder an unmodified system. Bounding each automated step with a timeout and escalating
  rather than retrying is what keeps the first option's downside finite.
</TradeOff>

<TradeOff title="Tight SLO vs. achievable SLO">
  A tight target surfaces real degradation and spends engineering time on reliability that users may
  not value. A loose one is cheap and never fires. The test is not what the team can hit but what
  users actually notice — an SLO that has never depleted its budget is measuring the wrong thing or
  is set too loose to be an instrument.
</TradeOff>

<TradeOff title="Quality as an SLI vs. availability and latency only">
  Availability and latency are free from the serving layer and miss the failure mode that matters
  most for AI systems — fast, well-formed, wrong output. A quality SLI catches it and is always a
  proxy, needs its own pipeline, and can itself regress silently. Having an imperfect quality signal
  beats having none, provided its limitations are stated.
</TradeOff>

## Cost

- **Telemetry retention is set by the longest burn-rate window**, so a 30-day budget window commits
  to 30 days of queryable resolution.
- **Alert-driven scaling spends money on a signal**, which makes a mis-tuned burn-rate rule a
  direct billing event as well as a paging one.
- **Warm headroom held for fast-burn response is standing cost**, justified by the incident latency
  it removes rather than by utilization.
- **Every automated remediation step has a cost when wrong** — a needless scale-up, a restart that
  drops in-flight work — which is why steps should be cheap and reversible before they are clever.
- **On-call is the largest cost in this architecture** and is spent by alert quality; noisy alerting
  is expensive in the least visible way.

## Observability

- **Burn rate per rule, with both windows exposed.** An alert that fired should show which window
  crossed and when, or it cannot be debugged after the fact.
- **Budget remaining as a signed quantity.** Clamping at zero discards the most useful
  information — how far over.
- **Severity as an explicit, queryable signal**, since it is the contract between alerting, scaling,
  and incident response.
- **Scaling decisions with their reason recorded** — proportional target, cooldown hold, or fast-burn
  override — so post-incident review can reconstruct why capacity moved.
- **Runbook step outcomes: resolved, timed out, escalated, exhausted**, per incident, which is what
  turns automation from a black box into something reviewable.
- **Time-to-detect and time-to-escalate**, the two intervals automation is supposed to compress and
  the only proof that it does.

## Production Deployment

<Checklist title="Before real traffic">
  - The SLO target and window were derived from user impact, and the derivation is written down.
  - Burn-rate rules use two windows per severity, and both are exported when an alert fires.
  - Alerting, scaling, and incident severity all read one computation, not three.
  - The autoscaler's cooldown has an explicit fast-burn override, and the override is tested.
  - Every runbook step has a timeout and a named escalation target.
  - Runbook steps are idempotent, verified by running one twice.
  - Exhausted automation reports an explicit terminal state naming who was last paged.
  - A quality SLI exists alongside availability and latency, with its proxy nature stated.
  - The SLI pipeline is load-tested to outlast the service it measures.
  - Automated remediation writes an audit trail equivalent to a human action.
</Checklist>

## Interview Questions

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="advanced" title="Why does burn-rate alerting need two windows rather than one threshold?">
  Because one window cannot satisfy both requirements. A short window pages on transient blips and
  then flaps; a long window misses most of a fast burn and keeps alerting after it stops, because
  the stale average has not decayed. Requiring a long and a short window to both cross the threshold
  gets the long window's noise suppression and the short window's fast clearing. The cost is
  configuration complexity, which is worth paying because alert fatigue and blindness are both
  worse.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="advanced" title="When should an autoscaler ignore its own cooldown?">
  When the error budget is burning at page severity. The cooldown exists to prevent flapping on
  noisy utilization samples — but a fast burn is not noise, it means users are being failed now, and
  waiting out a timer designed to suppress noise is the wrong response. Checking severity before
  checking cooldown gives a narrow, deliberate escape hatch while every other decision still
  respects it.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="intermediate" title="Why should alerting, scaling, and incident response read the same severity signal?">
  Because otherwise they can disagree, and they will disagree during exactly the incidents where
  clarity matters. Three subsystems reading three signals produce contradictory automation — scaling
  up while the incident is marked resolved, or paging while capacity is being removed. Deriving all
  three from one computation makes that class of confusion structurally impossible.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="advanced" title="Why should incident automation escalate rather than retry a failed step?">
  Because retrying assumes the step's execution failed, when far more often the step was the wrong
  remediation — a restart does not fix a capacity problem. Retrying burns the incident clock,
  produces no new information, and leaves no record of who was notified. Escalating to a named
  contact at each hop produces an audit trail and moves toward someone who can actually resolve it.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="intermediate" title="What should automation do when it runs out of runbook steps?">
  Report an explicit exhausted state naming the last contact escalated to. Looping or stopping
  silently withholds the fact the responder needs first — nobody has fixed this and it is now theirs.
  An honest terminal state is a feature; automation that hides its own failure is worse than no
  automation, because it delays the human response it was supposed to accelerate.
</InterviewQuestion>

<InterviewQuestion difficulty="advanced" title="Your SLO's error budget has never depleted. Is that good?">
  It is a warning. Either the target is set loose enough to be uninformative, or the SLI is not
  measuring what users actually notice. An error budget is an instrument, and one that always reads
  fine has not been calibrated against real impact. The corollary is that occasional budget
  depletion is expected and healthy — it is the signal the SLO exists to produce.
</InterviewQuestion>
